# Investment Chain Research Framework

## Core Idea

A theme becomes investable only when it can be connected to a durable profit pool, a measurable supply-demand imbalance, and a validation plan. The goal is not to produce an impressive story; the goal is to decide what to ignore, what to monitor, what to deep-dive, and what can become a testable investment hypothesis.

## Ten Questions

1. **What problem does the direction solve?**
   - Define the real-world constraint or job-to-be-done.
   - Examples: reduce token cost, increase compute supply, automate workflow, remove power bottleneck, improve yield, reduce labor cost.

2. **How should the industry chain be segmented?**
   - Segment by causal dependency, not by buzzword.
   - A useful chain starts with upstream bottlenecks and ends with demand monetization.
   - Default pattern: inputs/equipment -> manufacturing/packaging -> core component -> infrastructure -> platform -> model/API -> application -> customer.

3. **Who are the core players?**
   - List global and local players separately.
   - Separate leaders, challengers, suppliers, customers, and concept-only names.

4. **Which layer has the strongest profit pool and why?**
   - Look for scarcity, switching costs, IP, customer lock-in, capacity constraints, pricing power, and operating leverage.
   - Margin level alone is not enough; judge whether the margin is sustainable.

5. **Which layer is easiest to commoditize?**
   - Warning signs: low differentiation, open-source alternatives, transparent pricing, customer migration, supplier overcapacity, or cost-plus business model.

6. **What are the key variables?**
   - Choose variables that can change the thesis, not vanity indicators.
   - Good variables have clear directionality and observable data sources.

7. **What changed in the past 12-24 months?**
   - Identify whether the theme moved from capability race to commercialization, from shortage to oversupply, from narrative to orders, or from early adoption to retention.

8. **What is the mainstream narrative?**
   - State consensus plainly before trying to disagree with it.
   - Ask what is already priced in and what the market may be ignoring.

9. **What might be underestimated?**
   - Examples: demand is larger than expected, bottleneck lasts longer, second-line suppliers gain share, local substitution creates a protected profit pool, or application margin improvement is not reflected in valuations.

10. **What data should be checked next?**
    - Convert every thesis into a validation queue: filings, transcripts, orders, prices, shipments, capacity, product benchmarks, customer metrics, and policy.

## Initial Judgment Labels

| Label | Use when | Required next action |
|---|---|---|
| discard | no durable profit pool, evidence is mostly narrative, or obvious overpricing | record why it failed and stop unless new evidence appears |
| watch | logic is plausible but data is insufficient or timing unclear | define 3-5 variables and update cadence |
| deep-dive | evidence points to a real profit pool or variant perception | build company-level model, compare peers, verify valuation |
| investment hypothesis | there is a testable thesis with beneficiaries, catalysts, variables, and falsifiers | write a thesis memo and monitor disconfirmation |

## Profit Pool Tests

A layer is attractive when several of these are true:

- Customer demand is growing and budget is visible.
- Supply is constrained or hard to replicate.
- The product is mission-critical and switching costs are high.
- Gross margin and cash conversion are strong or improving.
- Unit economics improve with scale.
- The layer captures value rather than merely passing through costs.
- Competitors cannot quickly copy capacity, IP, data, distribution, or customer relationships.

## Commoditization Tests

A layer is vulnerable when several of these are true:

- Capacity can be added quickly.
- Customers can multi-source easily.
- Pricing is transparent and frequently renegotiated.
- Open-source or internal substitutes are improving.
- The layer is capital intensive but lacks pricing power.
- Revenue growth requires heavy capex or working capital with uncertain ROIC.

## Falsification Checklist

Always include falsifiers. Examples:

- Demand metric slows while capacity keeps expanding.
- Pricing declines faster than cost reductions.
- Gross margin falls despite revenue growth.
- A bottleneck shifts away from the favored layer.
- Customer adoption does not convert into paid usage or retention.
- A new regulation/export control blocks supply or demand.
- The supposed local substitution story lacks orders or margin.
- Competitors announce capacity that removes scarcity sooner than expected.
